ACT I · THE LANGUAGE OF ECONOMICS

# What Exactly Is Economics?

**Economics** studies how individuals, firms, governments and societies **allocate scarce resources among competing uses**.

1. **Scarcity:** resources are limited relative to wants
2. **Choice:** using a resource one way means not using it another way
3. **Allocation:** deciding what is produced, how it is produced and for whom

> **Common misconception:** Economics is not only about money, banking or the stock market. A farmer dividing land between crops, a student dividing time between subjects and a state dividing its budget between schools and roads are all making economic decisions.

# Every Economy Must Answer Three Central Questions

| Question | What it decides |
|---|---|
| **What to produce?** | Which goods and services, and in what quantities |
| **How to produce?** | Which techniques: more labour, more machines, or a mix |
| **For whom to produce?** | How the output is shared among people |

**One village, three linked answers (hypothetical):** A village with limited land and labour decides to grow more rice (*what*). Using tractors rather than hired workers (*how*) changes who earns wages from the harvest, and so who can afford to buy the rice (*for whom*). Change one answer and the others change too.

ACT I · THE LANGUAGE OF ECONOMICS

# Microeconomics versus Macroeconomics

| | Microeconomics | Macroeconomics |
|---|---|---|
| **Studies** | Individual economic agents and specific markets | The economy as a whole: aggregate outcomes |
| **Focus** | Households, firms, a single market, specific prices | Total output, inflation, unemployment, economic growth |
| **Typical question** | How is the price of wheat determined? | Why does the general price level rise? |

**Connecting the two:** A rise in diesel prices begins in one market (micro). If it raises transport costs for many firms and prices rise across the economy, it shows up in the general price level (macro).

> The distinction is individual units versus aggregates, not small businesses versus large ones. Even a very large firm is studied in microeconomics.

# Who Decides? Three Ways to Allocate Resources

| | Market economy | Centrally planned economy | Mixed economy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Who answers what, how and for whom?** | Buyers and sellers, through prices | A central planning authority | Markets and government together |
| **Ownership of resources** | Mostly private | Mostly state | Both private and public |
| **Main strength** | Responds quickly to what buyers want | Can direct resources to national priorities | Combines market incentives with public provision |
| **Main weakness** | People with little purchasing power may be left out | Weak incentives and slow information | Getting the balance right is difficult |

**India is a mixed economy.** Markets allocate most goods and services, while government provides public services, regulates markets and runs public sector enterprises. The two sectors interact constantly: private firms build public projects, and public enterprises compete in markets.

ACT I · THE LANGUAGE OF ECONOMICS

# Facts, Values and Economic Reasoning

**Positive statement: about what is**

"If bus fares rise, the number of daily bus passengers will fall."

It can be tested against evidence.

**Normative statement: about what ought to be**

"Bus fares should be kept low so that everyone can afford to travel."

It rests on a value judgement; evidence alone cannot settle it.

> **Ceteris paribus: "other things being equal".** To study one cause at a time, economists hold other relevant factors constant. To see how the price of tea affects tea purchases, we hold income, tastes and the price of coffee constant.

ACT II · SCARCITY, CHOICE AND OPPORTUNITY COST

# Scarcity Forces Choices

![One limited resource with competing uses](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_11_Competing_Uses.png)

- **Scarcity:** available resources (land, labour, capital, time) are limited relative to the wants they could satisfy.
- Wants keep growing, so resources can never meet all of them at once.
- Every individual, firm and government must therefore **allocate**: decide which uses come first.

> Scarcity applies to rich and poor economies alike. A richer society faces different choices, not an absence of choice.

---

ACT II · SCARCITY, CHOICE AND OPPORTUNITY COST

# Scarcity Is Not the Same as Shortage

| | Scarcity | Shortage |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | A general condition: resources are limited relative to wants | A market situation: quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the prevailing price |
| **Where** | Every economy, all the time | A particular market, at a particular price |
| **Can it disappear?** | No, though the choices it forces change | Yes, if the price adjusts or supply rises |
| **Example** | India's land and water must be shared among farming, industry, housing and nature | Train tickets for a festival weekend sell out at the listed fare: more people want seats than there are seats |

> Scarcity is permanent; a shortage arises at a particular price. We will draw shortages on a graph in Act V.

ACT II · SCARCITY, CHOICE AND OPPORTUNITY COST

# Choosing One Option Means Giving Up Another

A student has **6 free hours on Sunday** to split between mock tests and answer-writing practice.

| Hours on mock tests | Hours on answer writing |
|---|---|
| 6 | 0 |
| 4 | 2 |
| 2 | 4 |
| 0 | 6 |

- Every extra hour on one activity is an hour taken from the other: this is a **trade-off**.
- A limited public budget works the same way: more spending on one scheme leaves less for another, unless the budget itself grows.

> This list of possible combinations is a preview of the Production Possibility Curve in Act III.

ACT II · SCARCITY, CHOICE AND OPPORTUNITY COST

# Opportunity Cost: What Do We Give Up?

**Opportunity cost** is the value of the **next-best alternative forgone** when a choice is made.

![Decision tree for a study evening](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_14_Decision_Tree.png)

- Only the next-best option counts, not every alternative added together.
- **It is not the same as money spent.** A free lecture costs no money, but it still has an opportunity cost: the best alternative use of that time. Paying ₹500 for a test series costs the best alternative use of the ₹500 and of the hours it takes.

ACT II · SCARCITY, CHOICE AND OPPORTUNITY COST

# Incentives and Thinking at the Margin

- **Incentives** are rewards or penalties that change behaviour: a higher price for a crop encourages farmers to grow more of it; a late fee encourages people to pay bills on time.
- **Marginal thinking** compares the **additional benefit** of one more unit of an action with its **additional cost**.

**Should a shop stay open one extra hour? (hypothetical)**

| | Extra revenue | Extra cost (power, staff) | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekday evening | ₹800 | ₹500 | Stay open: benefit exceeds cost |
| Late Sunday night | ₹300 | ₹500 | Close: cost exceeds benefit |

> Keep going while the additional benefit exceeds the additional cost.

ACT III · THE PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE

# The Production Possibility Curve (PPC)

The **PPC** shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when it uses all its resources fully and efficiently.

**Assumptions**

- A **given quantity of resources**
- A **given level of technology**
- Resources can be **moved between** the two goods

![The production possibility curve](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_17_PPC_Introduced.png)

It turns scarcity, choice and opportunity cost into a single picture.

|---|---|---|

- From A to F, food output rises from 0 to 50 units while textile output falls from 100 to 20.

ACT III · THE PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE

# Reading the PPC: Three Types of Points

![Points on, inside and outside the PPC](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_19_Three_Points.png)

- **On the curve:** resources are fully and efficiently used. This is **productive efficiency**: more of one good requires less of the other.
- **Inside the curve:** **underutilisation**, such as unemployed workers or idle factories. More of both goods is possible.
- **Outside the curve:** **currently unattainable** with existing resources and technology. It may become attainable if productive capacity expands.

---

ACT III · THE PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE

# Productive Efficiency Is Not the Whole Story

![Productive and allocative efficiency on the PPC](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_20_Efficiency.png)

- **Productive efficiency:** output of one good cannot rise without reducing the other. Every point on the PPC, including P, Q and R, meets this test.
- **Allocative efficiency:** the combination produced best matches what society values most.
- Choosing between P, Q and R needs a further judgement about society's preferences and objectives. Being on the frontier is necessary for allocative efficiency, but it is not enough.

ACT III · THE PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE

# Opportunity Cost on the PPC

![Moving from C to D along the PPC](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_21_Opportunity_Cost_PPC.png)

Moving from **C (20 food, 85 textiles)** to **D (30 food, 70 textiles)**:

- **Gained:** 10 units of food
- **Sacrificed:** 15 units of textiles
- Opportunity cost of the extra 10 food = **15 textiles**, or **1.5 textiles per unit of food**

> This is a movement along the curve: existing resources are reallocated, not increased.

---

ACT III · THE PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE

# Why Is the PPC Usually Bowed Out?

![Rising opportunity cost along the PPC](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_22_Bowed_Out.png)

- Each extra 10 units of food costs more textiles than the last: 5, then 10, 15, 20 and 30. This is **increasing opportunity cost**.
- **Why:** resources are not equally suited to every use. The first land and workers moved into food are those best at farming and least useful in textile mills. Later moves take resources that are far better at textiles, so more textile output is lost.
- This rising cost bends the curve outward, making it concave to the origin.

---

ACT III · THE PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE

# Moving Along the PPC versus Shifting It

![Movement along the PPC, an outward shift and an inward shift](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_23_Movement_vs_Shift.png)

- **Movement along the curve:** the same resources are reallocated between the two goods. Capacity is unchanged.
- **Outward shift:** productive capacity expands through better technology, more or better-skilled workers, more capital or newly usable resources.
- **Inward shift:** productive capacity is lost, for example when war or a major natural disaster destroys factories, infrastructure or farmland.

---

ACT III · THE PRODUCTION POSSIBILITY CURVE

# The PPC and Economic Growth

![Today's frontier and a future frontier](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_24_PPC_Growth.png)

- **Economic growth** pushes the frontier outward: combinations such as **Y**, unattainable today, become attainable.

ACT IV · THE CIRCULAR FLOW OF INCOME

# An Economy Is a System of Connected Flows

![The simplest circular flow of income](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_25_Income_Loop.png)

- Firms pay **factor incomes** to households for the factors they hire.
- Households **spend** that income on goods and services produced by firms.
- That spending becomes firms' revenue, which pays the next round of incomes. Income keeps circulating.

The **circular flow of income** is the simplest model of how an economy fits together.

---

ACT IV · THE CIRCULAR FLOW OF INCOME

# Households and Firms: Two Roles Each

**Households**

- **Own** the factors of production: land, labour, capital and entrepreneurship
- **Supply** factor services to firms and **receive** factor incomes
- **Consume:** buy goods and services with that income

**Firms**

- **Hire** factor services from households and pay for them
- **Produce** goods and services
- **Sell** output to households and receive sales revenue

> Production creates income, and income pays for production. In real life one person plays both roles: a factory worker earns wages from a firm and spends them in other firms' shops.

---

ACT IV · THE CIRCULAR FLOW OF INCOME

# Product Markets and Factor Markets

![Households and firms connected through two markets](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_27_Product_Factor_Markets.png)

- **Factor market:** where productive resources are hired, such as the labour market and the market for land. Households sell factor services; firms buy them.
- **Product market:** where final goods and services are bought and sold. Firms sell; households buy.
- The green arrows are **real flows**: what actually moves between households and firms.

# Real Flows and Money Flows Move in Opposite Directions

![Real flows and money flows in the circular flow](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_28_Real_vs_Money_Flows.png)

- **Real flows (green):** factor services from households to firms; goods and services from firms to households.
- **Money flows (gold):** factor incomes from firms to households; consumer spending from households to firms, which becomes firms' revenue.

# Expanding the Model: Three More Participants

![Financial system, government and rest of the world added to the circular flow](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_29_Expanded_Model.png)

# Leakages and Injections

![Injections add to the flow and leakages withdraw from it](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_30_Leakages_Injections.png)

| Leakages: income withdrawn from the flow | Injections: spending added to the flow |
|---|---|
| **Savings (S):** income not spent on consumption | **Investment (I):** firms' spending on capital goods |
| **Taxes (T):** paid to the government | **Government purchases (G):** of goods and services |
| **Imports (M):** spending on foreign output | **Exports (X):** foreign spending on domestic output |

> When injections exceed leakages, the flow of income tends to expand; when leakages exceed injections, it tends to contract.

# Understanding Demand

- **Demand:** the quantities of a good that buyers are **willing and able** to buy at different prices **during a given period**, other things being equal.
- **Quantity demanded:** the amount buyers want at **one specific price**.
- A **demand schedule** lists price and quantity pairs; plotting them gives the **demand curve**.

| Price (₹ per kg) | Quantity demanded (tonnes per week) |
|---|---|
| 50 | 20 |
| 40 | 40 |
| 30 | 60 |
| 20 | 80 |
| 10 | 100 |

![A downward-sloping demand curve](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_32_Demand_Curve.png)

*Hypothetical market for tomatoes. Price is on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis.*

ACT V · DEMAND, SUPPLY AND MARKET EQUILIBRIUM

# The Law of Demand

**Other things being equal, when the price of a good falls, the quantity demanded rises; when the price rises, the quantity demanded falls.** Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions.

**Why?**

- **Cheaper than substitutes:** buyers switch towards the good (substitution effect)
- **Income goes further:** the same budget buys more (income effect)
- **New buyers:** people priced out before can now afford it

**Everyday example:** when tomatoes fall from ₹40 to ₹30 per kg, households buy more. In our schedule, quantity demanded rises from 40 to 60 tonnes a week.

> **Careful:** a change in the good's own price changes the **quantity demanded** (a movement along the curve). It does not change **demand**.

# Movement Along the Curve versus a Shift of the Curve

# What Can Shift Demand?

| Factor | How it shifts demand | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Income** | Normal good: demand rises with income. Inferior good: demand falls as income rises | Rising incomes raise demand for air travel; some households buy fewer low-quality, unbranded staples and switch to better ones |
| **Preferences** | A stronger preference raises demand | A health campaign makes millets more popular |
| **Price of a substitute** | A dearer substitute raises demand | Coffee prices rise, so demand for tea increases |
| **Price of a complement** | A dearer complement lowers demand | Petrol prices rise, so demand for petrol cars falls |
| **Expectations** | An expected price rise raises demand today | Buying gold before an expected price increase |
| **Number of buyers** | More buyers raise demand | A new college raises demand for nearby rented rooms |

> An increase in demand shifts the curve to the **right**; a decrease shifts it to the **left**.

# Understanding Supply

- **Supply:** the quantities producers are **willing and able** to offer for sale at different prices during a given period, other things being equal.
- **Law of supply:** other things being equal, a higher price leads to a larger **quantity supplied**. Price and quantity supplied move in the **same direction**, so the supply curve usually slopes **upward**.
- **Why:** a higher price makes extra output worthwhile even as its cost rises, and draws in more sellers.

| Price (₹ per kg) | Quantity supplied (tonnes per week) |
|---|---|
| 10 | 0 |
| 20 | 30 |
| 30 | 60 |
| 40 | 90 |
| 50 | 120 |

![An upward-sloping supply curve](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_36_Supply_Curve.png)

**Producer example:** at ₹40 per kg, tomato growers near the city send far more of their crop to market than at ₹20.

ACT V · DEMAND, SUPPLY AND MARKET EQUILIBRIUM

# What Can Shift Supply?

![Movement along a supply curve and a shift of the supply curve](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_37_Movement_vs_Shift_Supply.png)

| Factor | Effect on supply |
|---|---|
| **Input costs** rise (seeds, fertiliser, wages, fuel) | Decreases: curve shifts left |
| **Technology** improves | Increases: curve shifts right |
| **Taxes** on production / **subsidies** to producers | Taxes decrease supply; subsidies increase it |
| **Number of sellers** rises | Increases |
| **Expectations** of higher future prices | Sellers may hold back stock: current supply decreases |
| **Natural conditions** | A good monsoon increases farm supply; drought or unseasonal rain decreases it |

**Indian example:** better seed varieties raise rice supply. At ₹30 per kg, quantity supplied rises from 60 to 90 tonnes. A change in the good's own price only causes a **movement along** the curve.

# Finding Market Equilibrium

![Equilibrium and a new equilibrium after demand rises](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_38_Equilibrium.png)

- **Equilibrium** is where **quantity demanded equals quantity supplied**: here **₹30 per kg and 60 tonnes a week**.
- At that price every buyer willing to pay ₹30 finds a seller, and no unsold stock is left over.
- **If demand increases** (say, in a festival season), demand shifts right: equilibrium price rises to ₹36 and quantity to 78 tonnes.
- In the standard model, an **increase in supply** with demand unchanged lowers the equilibrium price and raises the equilibrium quantity.

ACT V · DEMAND, SUPPLY AND MARKET EQUILIBRIUM

# Shortage, Surplus and Market Adjustment

![Shortage below equilibrium and surplus above it](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ArjunAgrawal101/ib-economics/presentation-assets/presentation-assets/upsc-economics-first-principles/png/UFP_39_Shortage_Surplus.png)

| | Shortage | Surplus |
|---|---|---|
| **Price** | Below equilibrium (₹20) | Above equilibrium (₹40) |
| **Market** | Quantity demanded 80 exceeds quantity supplied 30 | Quantity supplied 90 exceeds quantity demanded 40 |
| **Pressure** | Buyers compete; sellers raise prices | Unsold stock builds up; sellers cut prices |
| **Adjustment** | Price rises: quantity demanded falls, quantity supplied rises | Price falls: quantity demanded rises, quantity supplied falls |

> In a competitive market these pressures **tend** to move the price towards equilibrium. Real markets can adjust slowly because of time lags, contracts, storage limits and incomplete information.
